Large language models (LLMs) routinely annotate items with hierarchical labels, and deploying them means deciding which machine labels may enter a database unreviewed. Existing selective-annotation methods decide per item: an item is accepted with all its labels or reviewed in full. We study the label-level problem and propose HLCL. It scores each predicted node from the agreement of repeated LLM samples and sibling competition, accepts a prefix of each predicted path so that accepted labels are ancestor-closed, and certifies one threshold per depth with a learn-then-test procedure. The certificate bounds the label-level error ratio (wrong accepted labels over accepted labels) with probability 1−δ, needs only i.i.d. items, and uses a pooled betting test or an exact depth-wise test chosen on held-out data. Among certified thresholds, HLCL deploys the one with the lowest human effort under an explicit cost model. On four public taxonomies of depth two to four, two LLM annotators and 100 splits per setting, the realised error exceeded the target in at most 8% of splits. Compared with an item-level rule under the same certificate, deciding per label raised the share of correct labels accepted without review from 0.22 to 0.71 on the Blurb Genre Collection at α = 0.05. Against the strongest certified baseline in each setting, HLCL had lower mean effort in 14 of 20 settings; after correcting for overlapping splits it is significantly cheaper in 3, significantly more expensive in 1, and not significantly different in the rest.
